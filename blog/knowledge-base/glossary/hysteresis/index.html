Hysteresis

A system's response can depend on how its controls arrived at their present values.

Hysteresis means that a system’s response depends on the history of its controls, so the present control value alone does not determine the response. Turning a control up and then down can follow different response paths. The retained difference is a form of memory.

A household thermostat is a familiar case. Suppose it turns the heat on at or below 19 °C and off at or above 21 °C. At 20 °C, the heat is on if the room has been warming from 19 °C or below, and off if it has been cooling from 21 °C or above. The current temperature alone does not say which.

The ideal, rate-independent case depends on the path taken, not on how quickly it is traversed. The term dynamic hysteresis is also used for rate-dependent responses, as in Bermúdez, Gómez and Venegas’s analysis of hysteresis models. A short-lived lag can therefore resemble persistent memory, so state the convention and the observation time.

A two-threshold example

Let \(u_n\) be a dimensionless control at step \(n\), and let \(s_n\) be a stored bit, either 0 or 1, after that step. This toy relay switches to 1 at an upper threshold, switches to 0 at a lower threshold, and otherwise retains its previous bit:

\[ s_n= \begin{cases} 0, & u_n\le -1,\\ 1, & u_n\ge 1,\\ s_{n-1}, & -1\lt u_n\lt 1. \end{cases} \]

The inequalities specify what happens at the thresholds themselves. The starting bit must be supplied if the first control lies between them. Physical circuits implement the same two-threshold principle; see Texas Instruments’ comparator with hysteresis reference design.

Start each trial with either bit. The first control forces the state, so the answer does not depend on that choice:

Control sequence Bit after writing Bit after returning to zero
\(-2,\ 0\) 0 0
\(+2,\ 0\) 1 1

Applying zero again changes neither result. The key test is the read at the shared control of zero. Reading at \(-2\) in one trial and \(+2\) in the other would show only that different controls give different outputs, which needs no memory at all.

Hysteresis, bistability, and finite evidence

Bistability means that two stable attractors coexist under the same fixed controls. An attractor draws nearby trajectories toward it; its basin of attraction is the set of starting states whose trajectories approach it. The two attractors can be stable fixed points or stable repeating cycles (periodic orbits), not only two constant scalar values. Bistability can support hysteresis when changing a control moves a trajectory from one basin into the other, so the system retains a history-dependent choice after the control returns. Coexistence alone, however, does not establish hysteresis: the proposed write histories must actually reach different attractors and produce distinguishable readouts at identical controls.

Conversely, two distinguishable traces over a finite interval establish only finite history dependence unless longer-lived stability is also supported. A transient, the part of a trajectory before its eventual behavior becomes clear, can be long.

For a numerical memory claim, record the write histories, the common controls used during retention and reading, the initial states, the observation duration, the readout rule, and the arithmetic precision. Test whether the distinction survives longer holds and modest perturbations before generalizing beyond the measured interval. None of these tests alone establishes secrecy, indefinite retention, or useful storage capacity.

A reported example in a language-model loop

The November note on hiding bits and logic in a language-model feedback loop reports an experiment that runs Qwen2.5-0.5B as a soft-token feedback loop, where each step feeds back a probability-weighted average of token representations as the next continuous input vector. Its two controls are the base-10 logarithm of temperature (\(\log_{10} T\)) and a scalar steering strength (\(\alpha\)) that scales a fixed internal activation-steering vector. At one shared hold-and-read control setting \(H\) inside that green overlap, the loop has two coexisting six-step cycles, labeled \(H_0\) (for stored bit \(0\)) and \(H_1\) (for stored bit \(1\)). To write an intended bit \(b\in\{0,1\}\), the reported protocol moves the controls from \(H\) out to a write endpoint \(E_b\) and back to \(H\). On the way out and on the way back, the controls pass the same intermediate setting \(P_f = H + f\cdot(E_b - H)\) twice. Each group of runs tests all 4 combinations of starting cycle (\(H_0\) or \(H_1\)) and write endpoint (\(E_0\) or \(E_1\)), and the note reports 4 such groups (16 outward-and-return comparisons in total): the midpoint \(f = 0.50\) with 240 updates per stage (4 rows), the midpoint \(f = 0.50\) with 480 updates per stage (4 rows), \(f = 0.25\) with 480 updates per stage (4 rows), and \(f = 0.75\) with 480 updates per stage (4 rows).
